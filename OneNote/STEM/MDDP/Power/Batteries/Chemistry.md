Solid-State
 
- Solid electrodes and electrolyte

# Structure

- Electrolytes
	- Ceramics
		- Oxides
			- Glass
		- Sulphides
		- Phosphates
	- Solid polymers
- Cathodes
	- Lithium cobalt oxide
- Anodes
	- Vary more
	- Affected by type of electrolyte

	- $1670   m A h g^{- 1}$

# Advantages

- Higher energy density
	- Lithium metal anodes
- Avoid dangerous or toxic materials
	- Organic electrolytes
- Liquid electrolytes typically flammable
	- Solid non-flammable
	- 20-30% heat generation
- Faster charging
- Higher voltage
- Longer life cycle

- Energy & power density
- Durability
	- Lithium Dendrites

		- Short circuits

		- Electrodeposition during charge and discharge
- Material costs
	- Vacuum deposition
- Sensitivity
	- Low temperature ops may offer poor performance
	- Ceramic electrolytes need high pressure
		- Maintain contact with electrodes
	- May break from mechanical stress
- Stability

Nickel-Cadmium  
NiCd
 
- Mostly replaced by nickel-metal hydride
- Mature
	- Well understood

# Prefers

- Slow charge
- Pulse charge over DC
- Deep discharge
	- Periodic full discharge important
		- Memory effect

# Advantages

- Long life
- High discharge rate
	- Lots of cycles
- Price
- Rugged
	- Forgiving

# Limitations

- Cadmium is toxic
	- Environmentally unfriendly
- Low energy density
- Memory effect

# Applications

- Two-way radios
- Biomed equipment
- Professional video cameras
- Power Tools

Nickel-Metal Hydride  
NiMH
 
- Common consumer and industrial use
- Interim to Li-Ion

# Advantages

- Higher energy density than NiCd
	- 30 - 40%
	- Reduced cycle life
- Environmentally friendly materials
	- No Cadmium
	- Simpler storage
		- Less regulations
- Less prone to memory effect

# Limitations

- Less durable
- Cycle under heavy load
	- Performance deteriorates after 200 - 300 cycles
- Storage at high temperature bad
- Higher self-discharge
	- Higher than NiCd
- Longer charge time
	- More complex charge algorithm
	- Heat generation
	- Trickle charge critical

# Applications

- Wireless communications
- Mobile computing

Lead Acid
 
- Oldest
- Types
	- Technically the same
	- Sealed Lead acid
		- SLA
	- Valve regulated Lead acid
		- VRLA
- Low over-voltage potential
	- Stop it from reaching gas-generating potential

# Advantages

- Mature
- Inexpensive
- Economical for large power applications
	- Heavy
- Low self-discharge
- High surge currents
	- Relatively high power/weight ratio
- Best charge retention
- No **memory**

# Limitations

- Lowest energy to weight/volume ratio
- Store in **charged state**
	- Sulfation
- Doesn't like deep discharging
- 200 - 300 charge cycles
	- Grid corrosion of positive electrode
		- Worse when hotter
- Environmentally unfriendly
	- Lead
	- Transportation restrictions
- Thermal runaway
- SLA doesn't lend itself to **fast charge**
	- 8 - 16 hrs

# Applications

- Modern SLI applications
- Hospital equipment
- Wheelchairs
- Emergency lighting
- UPS systems

Lithium-Ion
 
- Sizes
	- Cylindrical
		- No demand for ultra-thin
	- Prismatic
		- Slim
			- \< 18mm
			- \< 4mm Li-ion polymer

# Advantages

- Best energy density
	- Lightweight
	- Double NiCd
- Low self-discharge
- High cell voltage
	- Batteries with one cell
	- High currents drawn
- Low resistance required
- Low maintenance
	- No memory
	- No scheduled charging
- Easier disposal

# Limitations

- Fragile
	- Requires protection circuit
		- Limits peak voltage of each cell during charge
		- Prevents from being too small on discharge
- Limited to 1C or 2C
- Rick of ignition from heat generated
- Aging
	- Some capacity loss after a year
		- Used or not
	- Can fail after 2 or 3 years
	- Cooling helps

Lithium-Ion Polymer
 
- Not replace Li-ion
- Dry solid polymer electrolyte
	- Plastic-like film
	- Not conducting
	- Allows ion transmission
	- Replaces porous separator soaked in electrolyte
- Thin
	- As low as 1mm
	- Main advantage

# Advantages

- Light
- Slightly higher energy density/Slight higher cost
- Any shape
- Simplification
	- Fabrication
	- Ruggedness
	- Safety
	- Thin-profile

# Limitations

- No capacity gains
- No cost advantage
- Risk of fire?
	- No because of dry electrolyte
- Poor conductivity
	- High internal resistance
	- Struggles with current bursts
	- High heat helps
	- Some gelled electrolyte added
		- Typically hybrid
		- Most batteries available

# Applications

- RC cars/boats/airplanes

Flow Battery
 
# Structure

- Ion exchange through membrane
- Voltage by Nernst equation
	- 1.0 - 2.43 V
- May be used like fuel cell
	- Spent fuel extracted and new stuff added
- Rechargeable battery
	- Power source drives regeneration of fuel
- Energy capacity a function of electrolyte volume
- Power function of surface area of electrodes
- Energy stored in electrolyte instead of electrodes

# Advantages

- Flexible layout
	- Separation of power and energy components
- Long lifecycle
	- No solid-to-solid phase transitions
- Quick response times
- No equalisation charging
	- Overcharging of battery to ensure all cells charged
- Low maintenance
- Tolerance to over(dis)charge
- Safe
	- Typically not flammable electrolytes

# Limitations

- Comparatively less powerful
- Low energy density
	- Large tanks
- Low charge/discharge rates
	- Large electrodes and membrane separators
		- Increase cost

# Applications

- Large scale
- Load balancing
	- Electrical grid
	- Off-peak hours
- Renewable storage
- Peak shaving
- UPS
- Power conversion
	- Charged with one number of cells
	- Discharged with a different number
		- Changes voltage
		- DC-to-DC converter
- Electric vehicles
	- Rapidly recharged
		- Replacing electrolyte
	- Low energy density
		- Short driving range
		- Except highly soluble Halates
- Stand-alone power system
	- No grid power available

# Redox
 
- Reversible cell
	- Rechargeable
- Heterogeneous electron transfer
	- Instead of solid-state diffusion or intercalation
	- _Fuel cells_

# Hybrid
 
- One or more electroactive components
	- Deposited as solid layer
- One battery electrode
- One fuel cell electrode
	- Limited in energy by surface area of electrode
- Types
	- Zinc-Bromine
	- Zinc-Cerium
	- Lead-acid
	- Iron-salt
- Good reversibility

# Membraneless
 
- Laminar flow
- Two liquids pumped through channel
	- Parallel with little mixing
- Membranes most expensive part
	- Least reliable
	- Corroded by repeat exposure
- Allows liquid bromine and hydrogen
	- Can't do with membrane
		- Hydrobromic acid ruins it
